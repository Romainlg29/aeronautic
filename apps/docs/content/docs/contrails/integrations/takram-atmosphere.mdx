<Example name="atmosphere-contrails" />

A contrail is white because its ice scatters, so under
[`@takram/three-atmosphere`](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@takram/three-atmosphere)
it should scatter the atmosphere's own light. Bring the sun down: the
sunlight through more air reddens and dims, the sky darkens, and the trails
follow both, at the exposure the sky meters. Change the altitude and the sky
is the one at that height, as the trails are.

The setup, the volume pass with the aerial perspective as its backdrop and
the scene's place on the earth, is on [core's page](/docs/core/integrations/takram-atmosphere).
`<Contrails>` finds the volume pass through its `<VolumePassContext>`, as
the plumes and the vapor do, and draws over the atmosphere's backdrop.

## The light at the aircraft

The trails take the light as the vapor does: the sun as an irradiance,
scattered through the crystals' phase function, and the sky as a radiance,
the same from every direction. Takram's `getSplitScalarIlluminance` gives
both at the aircraft, from the tables the sky is drawn with.

- **The sun** is its `direct`: the solar irradiance through the air above.
- **The sky** is the mean of the dome above, E<sub>h</sub> / π from its
  horizontal illuminance, and the sunlit ground below.

```ts
<Contrails
  airframe={airframe}
  look={{
    sunDirection, // towards the sun, world space
    sunColor: lighting.sun,
    sunIntensity: 1,
    skyColor: lighting.sky,
    skyIntensity: 1,
  }}
/>
```

Both are already in the atmosphere's units, so the look's `exposure` stays
at 1 and the camera photographs the trails with the sky.

The light is read once, at the aircraft, for the whole trail. A trail sinks
with its wake, at most some 100 m behind the docs' fighter, and the light a
few hundred metres lower is the same to well under 1 %.

## The air in front of it

A trail is kilometres long, so the air between it and the camera is worth
working out. The trails get no aerial perspective of their own: at the
tropopause, with takram's defaults, the air's extinction is about
3.6 × 10⁻³ per kilometre in green and 8.4 × 10⁻³ in blue (Rayleigh at
e<sup>−11/8</sup> of its sea-level 1.36 × 10⁻², almost no aerosol above its
1.2 km scale height, and a little ozone). Five kilometres of it, the far end
of the example's trail, keep 98 % of the light in green and 96 % in blue. At
6 km, the bottom of the altitude slider, they keep 97 % and 93 %.

Seen from the ground, through tens of kilometres of the denser air below,
the trails would need it.
