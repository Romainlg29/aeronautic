<Example name="atmosphere-lights" />

The fighter on its approach, 300 m up with the gear down, under
[`@takram/three-atmosphere`](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@takram/three-atmosphere)'s
sky, every light on. The sun starts 4° below the horizon, in civil
twilight. Raise it and the camera stops down for the brighter sky, so the
same lights fade into it. Lower it and they stand out as the dusk deepens.

The setup, the volume pass with the aerial perspective as its backdrop and
the scene's place on the earth, is on [core's page](/docs/core/integrations/takram-atmosphere).

## In the volume pass

The atmosphere's aerial perspective draws the sky wherever the opaque scene
left nothing, so whatever is drawn over the sky in the scene itself is drawn
over again. `<Lights>` finds the volume pass through its
`<VolumePassContext>` and draws its beams and its glare there, over the
atmosphere's backdrop. The spot and point lights stay in the aircraft,
lighting the scene.

The pass has no depth of its own: the beams and the glare read the opaque
scene's depth from `pass.backdrop` instead, so the airframe still hides the
lights behind it. Without React, give `backdrop` to the `Lights` and put its
`volumes` in `pass.scene`: the glare goes in `volumes` too.

## The light units

The look's `exposure` is the scene's value for 1 cd/m². The atmosphere draws
one unit on screen as about 75 700 cd/m², so under it the exposure is its
`luminanceScale`, whatever the camera meters:

```ts
import { AtmosphereParameters } from "@takram/three-atmosphere/webgpu";

const exposure = new AtmosphereParameters().luminanceScale; // ≈ 1.3 × 10⁻⁵

<Lights look={{ exposure }} />;
```

The lights are in candelas, the exposure turning them into the
atmosphere's units. The camera's exposure is the atmosphere's: its meter
reads the sunlight and the skylight at the aircraft and opens up as the
sun goes down, as a photographer's would.

## The air in front of them

The pass's contents get no aerial perspective from the atmosphere. The
lights dim and redden through their own air, from the `air`'s visibility:
at its default 23 km, Koschmieder's 3.912 / V is 0.17 per kilometre, and the
example's few tens of metres keep all but a fraction of a percent of the
light. Takram's air at 300 m is clearer than that. Set `visibilityM` to
match it if the lights are seen from kilometres off. The beams scatter in
the same air, so they show more in a mist than on a clear night.
