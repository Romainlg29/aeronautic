[`@takram/three-atmosphere`](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@takram/three-atmosphere)
draws a physically based sky, sunlight and aerial perspective from
precomputed scattering tables. Three things tie it to the aeronautic
effects: where the volumes go in its frame, where the scene is on the earth,
and what one unit of light on screen means. This page sets up all three; the
[afterburner](/docs/afterburner/integrations/takram-atmosphere),
[wing vapor](/docs/wing-vapor/integrations/takram-atmosphere),
[contrails](/docs/contrails/integrations/takram-atmosphere),
[countermeasures](/docs/countermeasures/integrations/takram-atmosphere) and
[lights](/docs/lights/integrations/takram-atmosphere) pages light
their effect in it.

```bash
pnpm add @takram/three-atmosphere @takram/three-geospatial
```

Import from `@takram/three-atmosphere/webgpu` only. The package's main entry
is the WebGL one and needs `postprocessing` installed.

### On three r186

Takram 0.19 is built against three r184. Under r186 it needs two fixes, which
these docs carry as pnpm patches in the repository's `patches/`:

- `struct()` hands back the struct type behind a proxy, not a function
  holding it as `layout`, so takram reads `.layout.name` off `undefined`.
  Both packages fall back to the type itself.
- The scattering tables' radius was left unnamed. r186 declares it inside
  the first branch that reads it, so the rays that meet the ground read a
  radius of 0, and the ground drew black. Takram's patch declares it once,
  before the branches.

## The volumes go over the aerial perspective

Takram's `aerialPerspective(color, depth)` fogs the opaque scene by the air
between it and the camera, and draws the sky wherever the depth is clear.
Put it after the volumes and it draws the sky straight over every plume seen
against the sky, since a volume writes no depth. Put it in the volume pass's
`backdropNode` instead: the opaque scene is graded first, and the plumes and
the vapor are laid over the result, refracting it where they are hot.

```ts
import { volume_pass } from "@aeronautic/core";
import { aerialPerspective } from "@takram/three-atmosphere/webgpu";

const split = volume_pass(scene, camera, {
  backdropNode: ({ color, depth }) => aerialPerspective(color, depth),
});

pipeline.outputNode = split.output;
```

The volumes themselves get no aerial perspective. At sea level the air's
extinction is about 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ per metre in green (Rayleigh 1.35 × 10⁻⁵,
aerosols 4.4 × 10⁻⁶, takram's defaults), so a plume 500 m away keeps 99 % of
its light, and 98 % in blue. Seen from kilometres away it would need it.

## Where the scene is on the earth

The atmosphere wants the scene's frame in ECEF, the earth-centred one.
Put the scene's origin at the aircraft, with +X north, +Y up and +Z east,
from `@takram/three-geospatial`:

```ts
import { Ellipsoid, Geodetic, radians } from "@takram/three-geospatial";

Ellipsoid.WGS84.getNorthUpEastFrame(
  new Geodetic(
    radians(longitude),
    radians(latitude),
    flight.values.altitudeM,
  ).toECEF(),
  atmosphere.matrixWorldToECEF.value,
);
```

Its height is the flight's altitude, so the sky darkens and the ground falls
away as the altitude slider climbs, in the same air the plumes and the
vapor read from the flight. The sun is a direction in the same frame:

```ts
const sun = new Vector3(
  Math.cos(elevation) * Math.cos(azimuth), // north
  Math.sin(elevation), // up
  Math.cos(elevation) * Math.sin(azimuth), // east
);

atmosphere.sunDirectionECEF.value
  .copy(sun)
  .transformDirection(atmosphere.matrixWorldToECEF.value);
```

For the sun at a date and place, the main entry's `getSunDirectionECEF(date)`
does it, with `postprocessing` installed.

## One unit of light

The atmosphere draws in units of its `luminanceScale`: one unit on screen is
1 / luminanceScale, about 75 700 cd/m². Everything else in the frame is put
in those units, so one exposure photographs it all:

- **The plumes.** A plume's radiance is in units of a 2000 K blackbody's,
  about 4.8 × 10⁵ cd/m², so its exposure is 4.8 × 10⁵ × luminanceScale,
  about 6.3.
- **The vapor.** Takram's `getSplitScalarIlluminance` gives the sun's
  illuminance and the sky's at a point, from the same tables the sky is drawn
  with. A compute pass reads them at the aircraft. The sun's is the vapor's
  sun directly. Its sky is a radiance, the same from every direction: the
  dome above, at E<sub>h</sub> / π from its horizontal illuminance, and the
  sunlit ground below, at albedo × (E<sub>sun</sub> sin e + E<sub>h</sub>) / π,
  averaged.
- **The camera.** An incident-light meter at the aircraft, its dome
  pointing up. From θ off its axis the dome shows (1 + cos θ) / 2 of its
  face, so it reads E<sub>sun</sub> (1 + sin e) / 2, plus 1.5 E<sub>h</sub>
  from the sky and half the ground's exitance. The sun still counts a little
  below the level, as it should: from 12 km the horizon dips 3.5°, and a sun
  at −2° still lights the aircraft. A flat sensor held level would read only
  the twilight sky there, and overexpose the sunlit aircraft by some seven
  stops. Then EV₁₀₀ = log₂(E · 100 / 330), a hemispherical receptor's
  constant (ISO 2720 gives 320 to 540), and the exposure is
  1 / (1.2 · 2<sup>EV</sup> · luminanceScale). A clear noon meters about
  EV 15, sunny sixteen.
- **The lens.** The sun's disc is drawn at its own 1.6 × 10⁹ cd/m², so the
  glow round it is the lens's veiling glare, a few percent of the light
  (ISO 9358), and not a bloom's. Three's `bloom` adds its five blurred levels
  back at weights summing to 3 at a radius of 0.2, so a glare of 2 % is a
  strength of 0.02 / 3, a threshold of 0, and the image dimmed by the 2 % it
  scattered.

## The light

Install takram's light node once, add an `AtmosphereLight` for the sun, and
let `skyEnvironment()` light everything else. It leaves the sun out, so the
light's own sky term is turned off:

```ts
renderer.library.addLight(AtmosphereLightNode, AtmosphereLight);

const sun = new AtmosphereLight();
sun.indirect.value = false;
scene.add(sun);

scene.environmentNode = skyEnvironment();
```

Below the horizon takram's sky is only the air between the camera and the
ground, near black from a few hundred metres up. A metal skin seen from
below then reflects black ground hard against the bright horizon, and each
panel falls on one side of it or the other as the camera turns. The docs'
examples put the ground into the environment, lit as the aerial perspective
lights a surface: where a ray of the environment meets the ground, its albedo
under the sun and the sky there (`getSplitIlluminance`), over π, through the
air between (`getIndirectLuminanceToPoint`'s transmittance), added to
`environment.skyNode`. The environment is drawn again only when the sun or
the camera move far, so it costs nothing per frame.

The atmosphere is found through the renderer's context, by compute passes as
well as materials:

```ts
const atmosphere = new AtmosphereContext();
atmosphere.camera = camera;
// Its noise wants temporal antialiasing to smooth it
atmosphere.raymarchScattering = false;

renderer.contextNode = context({
  ...renderer.contextNode.value,
  getAtmosphere: () => atmosphere,
});
```

## The whole of it

The examples' `<Atmosphere>` does all of the above round its children, and
hands them the light at the aircraft through `useAtmosphereLighting()`.
